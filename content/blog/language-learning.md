## Learn English Grammar
To me, this step is heavily understated in the language learning community. You are about to drop yourself in the deep-end of a language you don't understand, and you need to make sense of why things are the way they are. Almost every explaination that you are going to find for "why did this word just change form" is going to include grammar jargon, so my advice is to learn that jargon in your native language. This is not something you need to study intensly, it should only take a maximum of a day or two, but learning all of these concepts through your foreign language the first time is not efficient.

For example, do you know why we don't say "he gave it to *I*" instead of "he gave it to *me*?" That is going to be a very important nuance when you are trying to learn a completely new set of personal pronouns in your foreign language. 

With that said, here are the grammar topics that you should be refreshing yourself on before you begin your journey in your desired foreign language:

- **Parts of speech:** noun, verb, adjective, adverb, pronoun, preposition, conjunction, article/determiner.
  - Why this matters: In Romanian, a common question for learners is "what is the difference between 'bun' (good) and 'bine' (okay/well)?" This can seem like a really daunting question when you find these two words without any grammatical background. However, you sort of already intuitevly know the answer -- which of these sentences sounds unnatural: "he runs so good" or "he runs so well?" It's the latter, beacuse you don't use an adjective to describe a verb, you use an adverb.

- **Sentence roles:** subject, direct object, indirect object. 
  - Why this matters: grammar cases (ex: nominative, accusative, dative) are just markings for these roles. When you see that specific pronouns map to specific grammar cases, the picture is already complete for you.
  
- **Person and number:** 1st/2nd/3rd person, singular/plural.
  - You likely already know most of what there is to know about this one, but one gotcha that surprises a lot of people is that the personal pronoun "we" is actually the 1st person plural.
  - Another tip: english doesn't have a dedicated 2nd person plural, so if you're learning a language that does "ya'll" is what maps to it if you're trying to Google Translate a phrase.

- **Gender and agreement:** English barely has these, but many languages require adjectives, articles and verbs to "agree" with a noun's gender and number.

- **Tense vs. aspect:** tense is *when* (past, present, future); aspect is *how* an action unfolds (completed vs. ongoing, e.g. perfective/imperfective, progressive, perfect).
  - English has a mind boggling amount of these.

- **Mood:** indicative (facts), imperative (commands), conditional ("would"), subjunctive (wishes, doubt, "if I were").
  - The subjunctive surprises a lot of English speakers. We do it weirdly which doesn't map well onto foreign languages.

- **Verb types:** transitive vs. intransitive, auxiliary and modal verbs, reflexive verbs ("I wash myself").

- **Non-finite forms:** infinitive, participle (present/past), gerund.

- **Clauses:** main vs. subordinate clauses, and relative clauses ("the book *that I read*").

This is the most boring, but one of the highest ROI things you can do. Don't overcomplicate this. Paste these topics into an AI prompt and just have it explain each of them briefly, with a couple example sentences for each.

## Decide whether you want an accent

This decision will greatly impact the amount of time it takes to learn your language. Many people don't even take this into consideration. There are many pros to having an accent in your target language that are often understated.

The biggest one is that native speakers will subconciously use more common words if they detect an accent. This is a measured phenomenon, and not because of any prejudices they have against you. This obviously makes for much easier conversation.

Really the only benefit to nailing a native accent is impressing people, but you will be signing yourself up for a world of pain. Me personally, I derive much joy from nailing pronounciation, but it's not something I would recommend to most for the following reasons:

1. It adds a significant amount of time to learning.
    - Saying each word out loud literally adds seconds to each interaction versus a split-second comprehension.
2. Native speakers get frustrated (which sort of nullifies the whole impressing thing)
    - That subconcious action in their head never fires. So when you tell them to slow down, they will gradually pick up speed until you have to tell them to slow down again.

Having an accent isn't a failure. We are not designed to recognize sounds that aren't in our native language. It takes meticulous practice if you want to get it right. This video explains it really well [Why you have an accent in a foreign language](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FcN3HnQz3y4).

## The alphabet

Since my method involves lots of reading, your first step is to memorize the sounds of the alphabet for your target language. The methods for this will vary for each language, but generally you should look for a video that goes through each letter individually, and then shows lots of example words.

This is a really good example of the format you're looking for: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=olQh39MoJsQ

Don't skip this step. Avoid just throwing yourself in the deep-end and letting LingQ read out each word to you with text-to-speech. It is very difficult for beginners to separate which sounds belong to each letter, so it merits a dedicated study sessions.

## A birds-eye view of the grammar

Depending on how you do this may be the difference between you quitting or not. Let me be clear: **DO NOT INTENSELY STUDY GRAMMAR BEFORE YOU'VE HAD LOTS OF EXPOSURE WITH THE LANGUAGE**. If you do this the wrong way around, you are making it very easy for yourself to give up, and it boils down to this: you cannot understand nuance at this point. You have no baseline, so trying to apply grammar rules to zero foundation is just going to lead to frustration.

There is where high school and college language courses fail lots of people. The reason this method isn't taught in schools is because theirs no way to grade it fairly.

So with that said, I recommend the grammar guides on [elon.io](https://elon.io/grammar)

Your job is to skim, and I really mean skim, the beginner grammar guides for your target language. If anything doesn't click after reading the sentence like one or two times, just skip onto another section. Below I'll provide some examples of what your takeaways should be, given a couple of different sections.


Source: https://elon.io/grammar/russian/adjectives/agreement/overview
> In the nominative, an adjective takes one of four ending-sets depending on the noun's gender (in the singular) or simply on whether the noun is plural:
> | Agrees with | Ending | Examples |
> |---|---|---|
> | Masculine sg. | -ый / -ий / -ой | но́вый, ма́ленький, большо́й |
> | Feminine sg. | -ая / -яя | но́вая, после́дняя |
> | Neuter sg. | -ое / -ее | но́вое, после́днее |
> | Plural (all genders) | -ые / -ие | но́вые, после́дние |

See what I mean about gammar jargon making it's way in there? But anyway, your takeaway here is in that first sentence: "In the nominative, an adjective takes one of four ending-sets depending on the noun's gender (in the singular) or simply on whether the noun is plural"

All you care about is that the adjectives have a certain set of shapes in the nomitative case. Right now you only care that they *do* change, and not what they change into. Your job right now is just to plant that seed of "oh I think I vaguely remember reading about this" when you finally see it in action.

Source: https://elon.io/grammar/german/adjectives/overview
> An adjective in German does one of two jobs.
> 
> It can stand after a linking verb — sein (to be), werden (to become), bleiben (to remain) — and describe the subject from a distance. This is the predicate position. Here the adjective is uninflected: it takes no ending whatsoever, no matter the gender or number of the noun it describes.
> 
> Or it can stand before a noun and modify it directly inside the noun phrase. This is the attributive position. Here the adjective must take an ending.

I don't remember the tinest bit of German grammar from high school, but my takeaway here is that a german adjective is *sometimes* inflected. I don't care when or why right now... just that it's something that happens. 

You can count on all of these question coming up again someday, but the difference is you actually have a real example sentence to connect it back to. That is the essence of grammar study, it needs to be personal to *your* journey.

Do not spend more than a day on this. You are not going to catch everything, and that is OK. You may even read an entire article in the beginner section and not catch on thing. That's fine. Just skim through each of the articles under the beginner (A1) sections and whatever comes out of it, comes out of it.

## The crux: LingQ reading

LingQ is going to be the foundation of your language learning. Their "Mini Stories" are an actual goldmine. That's where you are going to start. Sign up for their premium plan, because the free one is too limited for any amount of reasonable progress. Then go straight to the mini stories. This is step 1 of my pipeline: the exposure stage. You are going to be greeted with short stories describing different scenarios, from multiple perspectives. At this point, you will likely have zero known words, for the first lesson, just click on each word to see what it means. Keep clicking on the words, and reading the words in your target language so you can expose yourself to the new words.

This part is very important: do not repeat lessons. I don't care if you only understood three words out of that entire story. **Keep going**. The essence of LingQ is to keep exposing yourself to the language. This is going to really suck when you're first starting out, because you're going to understand almost nothing, but that's where the next step in the pipeline comes in - Anki. But before we get there, you need to get into the following habit in LingQ so you don't blow up the Anki step.

Every time you start a new lesson in LingQ, you need to navigate to the sidebar, and move every single "LingQ'd" word up **one** level. It doesn't matter whether that word has actually graduated to that level or not in your head (because you are also really bad at measuring that yourself). You are going to keep doing this until a word reaches level 3, at which point you should leave it alone until you actually understand it in a later read. Once you can see the word without clicking on it to see the english meaning, then set it to level 5. If you find yourself not making that jump between level 3 and 5 for a word, then it's time for a mnemonic assiciation, which I will detail in a later section.

The reason I give this advice is having to make that spot-check decision for every single word is unnecessarily taxing. Really all you need to be concerned with is whether you know the word or not, which is super apparent, whereas "how well do I know this word?" is not an important detail by any means.

So once you are done with a lesson in LingQ, it's time to bring all of your fresh unknown words into Anki. LingQ has a native feature to do this, but it's super lackluster in my experience. So I built a chrome extension to fill this gap: [Aminte](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/aminte-anki-cloze-cards-f/lhejbifkielekmlbibjapplojjiboahe)

## Aminte + Anki

This is the second stage of the pipeline: the learning stage. This is where all of the learning and mental connections actually happen. Anki hacks your memory by showing you the missing words in each sentence, and you fill in the blanks. This is where you can actually make the leap from not understanding the language (i.e. translating everything) to actually understanding the language as is. As you do your flash cards every day, you will notice that the reading becomes substantially easier.

## LingQ listening

This is what completes the pipeline. LingQ has something called the "active playlist", every lesson that you complete will get slapped into there. And your job is to just press the play button every single day, and listen to your lingq lessons. I recommend pairing this with walks every day, especially outside if you can, because walking past the same scenery every day reinforces what you learned on previous days.

With this pipeline, there is going to be lots of repitition. This might seem boring at first, but once you make it say halfway through the mini stories, there is actually going to be quite a bit of variety. And by the time you're halfway through the mini stories you are going to notice how much you understand the first mini story, which was absolutely gibberish to you before.

So yeah, once you get through the mini stories, there are going to be more curated courses that you get to choose from. Choose whatever is most interesting to you.
Eventually I'll make a blog post on what to do once you've reached an interemediate level, but if you stick to this schedule for 6 months, your going to be blown away by how much you understand.

If you are going to implement this method yourself, I recommend setting aside 2 hours a day. Anki will take up most of your time: ~1hr, then \~30 minutes per LingQ lesson, and ~30 of listening to LingQ lessons.


## Speaking

So far I've only covered the reading and listening portion of learning a language, but haven't covered the most important (and difficult) part of learning a foreign language: speaking.

My advice is don't be in a rush to speak. If you speak too early, you are going to solidify bad habits. I started speaking Romanian at around 3k known words in LingQ in Romanian, and that was too early. I would recommend starting at around 6k known words.

When you are ready to start speaking, hopefully you have someone in your life that speaks the langauge. If you do, great, start speaking with them. But if you have the means, I highly recommend paying a tutor at iTalki for a few lessons per week.

Some tips for Italki though:
1. You need to be a little forceful at times. Teachers will want to bust out their powerpoint to teach you all of the grammar rules while you're paying them $20/hour. This is not a good use of your time or money. Your goal is to speak to them, no matter how bad it is, and have them correct you at opportune moments. Ask them to note down whatever mistakes they catch on their computer, and when you find yourself at a good pause, ask them to go over it with you. Don't let them ramble on about why something is wrong unless you specifically ask them. What this really boils down to is, to get better at speaking, you have to speak, and every minute spent not speaking in these lessons is precious.
