There's no easy way to learn a language. You won't hear that from a YouTube video that's trying to sell you something, so this part is about realistic expectations.

## "Just learn like a baby"

Lots of people online say all it takes is a Lingopie subscription and vegetating on the couch, because that's how babies do it. Here's what I have to say about that:

![SpongeBob reading a scroll of what learning like a baby actually requires: 24 hours of free time a day, two adults to feed you and change your diapers, patient native speakers, praise for every sound, and about 3 years before intelligible phrases](/images/baby_meme.jpg)

I'd tune this advice out. It only slows you down.

## Duolingo

In my experience, Duolingo is good for learning a language's alphabet, and not much else. Once you know the letters, I'd uninstall it. [This article](https://www.lingq.com/blog/can-you-become-fluent-with-duolingo/) covers why it won't get you much further.

## How long it takes

Short answer: **at least a year.**

![Map of FSI language difficulty categories for English speakers: Category I (most Western European languages) 552–690 hours, Category II (e.g. German, Indonesian) ~828 hours, Category III (e.g. Russian, Hindi) ~1,012 hours, Category IV (Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, Korean) ~2,200 hours](/images/fsi.png)

The US Foreign Service Institute (FSI) estimates are the numbers the language learning community usually cites. How long a language takes mostly depends on how close it is to your native language. To estimate your own timeline, divide the hours for your language by how much time you'll spend per day. For example, Romanian is Category I, and 690 hours at 2 hours a day comes out to about a year.

Here are my Romanian stats in LingQ. I'm upper-intermediate:

![My all-time LingQ stats for Romanian: 399 hours of study time, 315 hours of listening, and 16,046 known words](/images/lingq_stats.png)

My study time (399 hours) plus my listening time (315 hours) comes to about 714 hours, which lines up with the FSI estimate. One caveat: I tracked my time outside LingQ and added it manually. If you only count what LingQ logs on its own, the number won't match your level as closely.

## Whether to care about an accent

This choice has a big impact on how long learning takes, and most people never think about it.

### Tonal languages don't give you a choice

In tonal languages like Mandarin, Cantonese, Vietnamese, and Thai, the pitch of a syllable changes what the word means. In Mandarin, *mā* (a high, flat tone) means "mother," while *mà* (a sharp falling tone) means "to scold." Get the tone wrong and you've said a different word, so pronunciation is part of the vocabulary itself. If you're learning one of these, you have to care about your accent from day one.

### For everyone else, it's a tradeoff

**The biggest cost is time.** Working on pronunciation prolongs the whole process substantially. Saying each word out loud adds seconds to every review, compared to a split second of just recognizing it, and that adds up over thousands of cards.

Keeping an accent also has real benefits that don't get talked about enough. When native speakers hear an accent, they subconsciously slow down and switch to simpler, more common words. Linguists call this *foreigner-directed speech*, and it's not prejudice ([here's a short explainer](https://global-lt.com/blog/how-native-speakers-can-enhance-listening-comprehension-for-non-native-speakers)). It makes conversations much easier. The flip side, in my experience: with a near-native accent, that slow-down never kicks in, so when you ask someone to slow down, they gradually speed back up until you have to ask again.

I personally love nailing pronunciation, so I chose to work on it, knowing it would take longer.

An accent isn't a failure. We aren't built to hear sounds that don't exist in our native language, and getting them right takes meticulous practice. [This video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FcN3HnQz3y4) explains why really well.
